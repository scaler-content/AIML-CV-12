01

Generative vs. discriminative models

Keep expectations realistic

A GAN does not understand a character in the human sense, and it is not guaranteed to create a useful or unique design: it learns statistical patterns from its training data. This is a teaching-scale experiment, not a production-quality anime generator.

Explorer · Three model types

Check yourself: how does a generative model differ from a discriminative one? Give one vision example of each.

A discriminative model predicts a target from an input (image → cat/dog). A generative model learns patterns in the data distribution and can produce new samples (random latent vector → synthetic image).

02

Two networks in competition

A GAN has two networks. The generator (G) maps a random latent vector z to a synthetic image. The discriminator (D) receives a real or generated image and estimates whether it is real. D is trained to tell them apart; G is trained to make its samples receive a “real” prediction. It is often described as a cat-and-mouse game.

“Adversarial” means D learns from two kinds of examples (real images → real, generated images → fake), while G receives gradients through D and adjusts its parameters so its images are more likely to be classified as real. G does not “want” anything: it simply follows gradients.

Dataset preparation: anime faces

The huggan/anime-faces dataset provides 64×64 anime-face images; its dataset card notes some poor crops and non-human faces. It is a single training split, with no labels. For a manageable lesson the notebook streams 3,000 images (stopping with an error if fewer than 256 are read) and builds a DataLoader with batch size 64, shuffling, and drop_last.

Preprocessing: convert to RGB, resize to 64×64, convert to tensors in [0, 1], then normalize to [−1, 1]. Why? The generator ends in tanh, whose output range is [−1, 1]; matching the real-image range keeps discriminator inputs consistent.

The range of the real data must match the generator's output activation. Otherwise the discriminator can separate real from fake by numeric scale instead of image structure.

04

Discriminator: a real-vs-fake CNN

The discriminator takes a 3 × 64 × 64 image and downsamples it with strided convolutions. DCGAN-style design: Conv2d to learn spatial features and shrink the map; LeakyReLU to keep a small gradient for negative activations; BatchNorm2d in intermediate layers (not necessarily every layer); and a final scalar logit.

Visualizer · Walk the discriminator

Logit, not probability

The loss is BCEWithLogitsLoss, which fuses sigmoid and binary cross-entropy in a numerically stable way, so the discriminator returns a raw logit. Apply a sigmoid only to display a probability. Its task is binary: “real training sample” vs. “generated sample”; it does not identify character categories.

05

Generator: noise to image

The generator starts from a latent vector of length 100, treated as a 100 × 1 × 1 map, and uses ConvTranspose2d layers (a learned upsampling) to grow it to 3 × 64 × 64. Intermediate layers use BatchNorm2d and ReLU; the last layer uses Tanh to produce RGB values in [−1, 1].

Visualizer · Walk the generator

A test batch of 8 latent vectors produced a tensor of shape (8, 3, 64, 64) with values from −0.9996 to 0.9999, all inside the tanh range. An untrained generator's images look like noise.

06

The adversarial objective and training order

With x a real image, z a latent vector and D(·) a logit, both losses use binary cross-entropy with logits:

LD = BCE(D(x), 1) + BCE(D(G(z)), 0)
LG = BCE(D(G(z)), 1)

The generator loss is the commonly used non-saturating objective: it keeps providing a useful learning signal when the discriminator initially rejects generated samples strongly.

Training order

Stepper · One training iteration

Why detach during the D update? fake.detach() stops gradients flowing back into G during that step, so only D's parameters change. Why not detach during the G update? G needs gradients that pass through D to reach its own parameters; D's weights are frozen with requires_grad=False, but its operations stay in the computation graph.

Optimizers: Adam, learning rate 0.0002, betas (0.5, 0.999) for both networks; loss BCEWithLogitsLoss; a fixed noise batch of 64 latent vectors is reused every epoch to compare progress.

07

Train, monitor and read the losses

The run trains for 5 epochs on the 3,000 images (a GPU is strongly recommended; about 0.5 minutes in the recorded run). After each epoch the generator renders the fixed noise batch, so the same latent vectors can be compared over time; a GIF and TensorBoard scalars record progress. Do not expect a polished face after only a few epochs.

Reading GAN losses carefully
  • The networks compete: one loss can rise while the other falls.
  • A low discriminator loss is not automatically good news: D may be overpowering G.
  • A low generator loss does not prove images are diverse or realistic.
  • Always inspect sample quality and diversity next to the curves. Never use one loss value as a GAN quality score.
